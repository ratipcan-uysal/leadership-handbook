# Appendix C
# Question Banks

Choose a question for the decision or conversation in front of you. Listen to the answer before selecting another. The aim is a better next step, not a completed questionnaire.

## How to use these banks

**Before you ask.** Name the purpose and choose a few relevant prompts. Say whether you are exploring, advising, collecting feedback, or making a decision. A question should not conceal a decision you have already made.

**After you listen.** Check that you understood the answer. Agree the next action, its owner, the support needed, and when to return to it. If no action is needed, say so. Record only the detail needed to follow through, in a place appropriate to its sensitivity.

**Choose the bank.** Use Hiring for candidate evidence; One-on-Ones for regular check-ins; Coaching for a problem someone owns; Career Development for learning; Strategy and Problem-Solving for diagnosis and execution; Decision-Making for a choice; Creativity for new options; Listening and Dialogue for understanding; Self-Assessment for your own behavior; Conflict for competing interests; the Manager Event Loop for recurring commitments; and Measuring Yourself as a Manager for structured feedback.

**An illustrative follow-through.** A report says progress is blocked by a decision from another team. Confirm the decision needed, who can make it, and the consequence of delay. Agree who will contact that person and when the report will hear back. At the next check-in, establish whether the decision arrived and whether it removed the blocker. Do not close the item merely because a message was sent.

The questions retain the existing handbook's coverage and add selected material from the Notion collection. The usage guidance is an editorial adaptation, not a claim that the source authors tested this protocol. Source details appear at the end of this appendix.

## Hiring

**Use when.** Before an interview, choose the questions that test the role requirements you have already agreed. Use the same core prompts across candidates; use follow-ups to clarify their evidence.

**After the answer.** Ask for the situation, the candidate's own contribution, the result, and what they learned. Record what the answer demonstrates against the role criteria before comparing candidates. A polished story is a lead to investigate, not proof of competence. Treat the outside-work question as optional rapport, not a measure of fit.

**Keep in mind.** An unclear answer needs a concrete example. A weak example needs another relevant piece of evidence, not a personality label. See Chapter 03 for the hiring decision.

### Questions

- Among the people you've worked with, who do you most admire and why?

- What do you want to do differently in your next role?

- Tell me about a time you took unexpected initiative. Follow-up: Can you tell me about another?

- When was the last time you changed your mind about something important?

- Why shouldn't we hire you?

- What have I not asked you that I should have?

- For the last few companies you've been at: when you left, why did you leave? When you joined the next one, why did you choose it?

- Imagine yourself in three years. What do you hope will be different about you then compared to now?

- What's something outside of work you're irrationally passionate about?

- What would your previous manager say is the area you most need to grow in?

> Source: Existing handbook bank; Notion Interview Questions, drawing on First Round Review.

## One-on-Ones

**Use when.** At regular check-ins, let the report choose what matters first. Select an operational, developmental, or relational question to match what they need that day; do not work through the whole list.

**After the answer.** If a blocker depends on you, name the action you will take and when you will report back. If the person needs space to think, ask whether they want listening, coaching, or a decision. Before closing, confirm what may be shared and revisit the agreed action at the next check-in.

**Keep in mind.** A repeated "fine" is not evidence that nothing is wrong. Offer a more specific question and room to decline; do not force a personal disclosure. See Chapter 08 for the conversation structure.

### Questions

- What's the most important thing you're working on right now, and how is it going?

- What's getting in your way?

- What's something you wish I knew that I might not?

- What's one thing I could do differently that would make your job easier?

- Where are you on the development goals we set together?

- Are you enjoying the work? What's most and least energizing right now?

- Do you feel like you're being challenged enough? What would stretch you more?

- Is there anything unsaid between us that we should talk about?

- What would you do if you were in my position?

- How can I support you better?

- What's something frustrating you right now that I might not be aware of?

- What do you think?

- Do you understand what I'm asking you to do - and does it make sense to you?

- How can I improve my communication with you?

- What's next for you?

> Source: Existing handbook bank; Notion one-on-one notes and the Ron Edmondson questions reproduced in its Leading with Questions summary.

## Coaching

**Use when.** When someone owns a problem and needs help thinking it through. Establish whether they want coaching; if they need a decision only you can make, make that responsibility explicit.

**After the answer.** Separate an observation from an explanation. If the cause is uncertain, agree what to check before choosing a fix. If several options remain, compare their consequences and select a next step with an owner and a review point. Let the person articulate that step in their own words.

**Keep in mind.** Work from understanding to options to commitment. When the answer reveals a missing skill, resource, or decision right, provide it; more questions alone will not remove that constraint.

### Questions

- What do you see as the underlying root cause of the problem?

- What options, potential solutions, or courses of action are you considering?

- What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?

- What would the worst possible outcome be? What's the most likely outcome?

- What have you already tried?

- What is your initial inclination for the path you should take?

- Is there another solution that isn't immediately apparent?

- Is this an either/or choice, or is there something you're missing?

- What would happen if you did nothing at all?

- Is there anything you might be explaining away too quickly?

- How would you define success in this scenario - and how will you know?

- How do you feel about the project thus far?

- What have you accomplished so far that you are most pleased with?

- How would you describe the way you want this project to turn out?

- Which of these objectives will be easiest to accomplish? Which will be most difficult?

<!-- pagebreak -->

### Coaching (continued)

- What key things need to happen to achieve the objective? What support do you need?

- Can you more fully describe your concerns?

- How would you describe the current reality?

- What are a few options for improvement?

- What will you commit to do - and by when?

- What resources have we never used?

- What do we expect to happen if we do that?

- What is stopping us?

- How do you think we might find the answer?

- What's been the best part of your week so far?

- Which uncertainty is making this problem hardest to resolve?

- What is at stake for the people affected by this decision?

- What could we simplify without losing the result we need?

> Source: Existing handbook questions and additions from the Notion one-on-one list; Marilee Goldberg, Mark Harper and Ron Edmondson as reproduced in the Leading with Questions notes.

## Career Development

**Use when.** In a dedicated development conversation, after a stretch assignment, or when someone says they are no longer learning. Separate this discussion from an immediate status review.

**After the answer.** Use a concrete past learning experience to choose the next one. Agree a bounded assignment, the support or mentor needed, and a visible sign of progress. Set a review date and check both learning and workload; enthusiasm for growth is not consent to unlimited extra work.

**Keep in mind.** If the goal exceeds what the role can offer, say so and explore realistic routes. A preferred learning method is a starting hypothesis to test, not a fixed label for the person.

### Questions

- Where do you want to be in two years? In five?

- What do you want to learn while you're in this role?

- What aspects of your work do you find most energizing? Most draining?

- What would a stretch assignment look like for you right now?

- What skills do you want to develop that you're not currently using?

- Who do you most want to be like in 10 years, and what are they doing that you're not doing yet?

- Are you happy in this role? What would make it better?

- What do you appreciate most about this team that you've never told anyone?

- What changes could we make that would improve your work life?

- Think of a time you learned something difficult well. What conditions helped: practice, observation, discussion, feedback, or something else?

- What did you do to create that learning opportunity, and which part could you repeat here?

- Who benefited from what you learned, and what changed in their work?

> Source: Existing handbook bank; additional learning-condition prompts adapted from Diana Whitney and colleagues as reproduced in the Notion Leading with Questions screenshots.

## Strategy and Problem-Solving

**Use when.** At the start of a planning cycle, when a team keeps treating symptoms, or when a plan has goals but no credible route to execution. Select the relevant questions below.

**After the answer.** For diagnosis, write down the competing explanations and the evidence needed to distinguish them. For direction, describe the future capability of the customer or team. For execution, name an accountable owner for each outcome, a communication owner, a measure, and a review date. Resolve missing authority or resources before calling the plan agreed.

**Keep in mind.** Do not treat an answer from the most senior person as customer evidence. Check the proposed benefit with the people affected. Review the plan when its key assumption changes, not only when its calendar review arrives.

### Questions

- What do we want to be true in a decade that is not true today?

- What is our current competitive advantage, and is it durable?

- What are the most pressing threats we will face over the next twelve months?

- What are we missing or forgetting?

- Where should we be placing our best energy?

- What would the most expansive way to state this problem be? What is the most pinpointed way?

- What have others done to solve this problem successfully?

- What do the people closest to this problem say about it?

- What metrics will we use to determine whether our intervention worked?

- How will we generate buy-in for the plan?

- What matters most right now?

- What is one problem I can turn into an opportunity?

- What is our customers' greatest pain?

- How will I be more strategic?

<!-- pagebreak -->

### Strategy and Problem-Solving (continued)

- How can I make swift yet smart decisions?

- What leadership skill can - and should - I get better at?

- How will I recognize success?

- What is my biggest fear, and how will I face it?

- What can we learn from this?

- How can we do it better next time?

- Which observations support our explanation of the problem, and which would point to a different cause?

- What should our customers be able to do in the future that they cannot do today?

- If an outside team examined this problem, what would it investigate first?

- Which planning horizon are we committing to, and what are the few outcomes that matter within it?

- Who owns each outcome, who needs to hear the plan, and who will explain it to them?

- What will be on the progress scorecard, and when will we decide whether to continue, change, or stop?

- What would a competing approach have to do to make our current model unnecessary?

- Which unanswered question about our future could change what we prioritize today?

> Source: Existing bank; Mark Miller problem-solving and strategy notes, the Powerful Questions Model image, and the Michael Schrage passage and Berger visionary-question image in Notion. New operational wording is an editorial adaptation.

## Decision-Making

**Use when.** Before committing to a consequential choice, especially when approval, fear, or attachment to one option is narrowing the discussion. Use privately first if the question concerns your own motives.

**After the answer.** Write the decision criteria before comparing options. Distinguish the downside you can recover from and the downside you cannot accept. Seek evidence that could change the choice. If uncertainty can be reduced by a small reversible step, define its limit and the result that would change your decision; otherwise identify who must decide and by when.

**Keep in mind.** Imagining that failure is impossible can generate options; it cannot justify taking a risk. Bring the real constraints and obligations back before committing. Use Chapter 14 for the broader calibration and pre-mortem discussion.

### Questions

- What would you do if you knew you could not fail?

- If you had to advise a friend facing the same decision, what would you tell them?

- How might you view this choice five years from now?

- What option might you be overlooking because it seems too unconventional?

- If approval from other people were not a factor, would I choose differently? Which obligations would still remain?

- Which value is guiding my choice, and what legitimate value does it put at risk?

- What outcome do I want to believe in, and what evidence would challenge it?

- If the current options disappeared, what other route could achieve the purpose?

- If this choice fails, what would recovery require, and is that cost acceptable?

- What is the smallest reversible step that would reduce the uncertainty?

> Source: Adapted from Notion Decision-Making and the Berger screenshots. See the source note for the distinction between his two books.

## Creativity

**Use when.** When the team is recycling the same proposals, after an unsuccessful attempt, or before choosing an approach to an unfamiliar problem. Separate generating options from choosing one.

**After the answer.** Capture the alternatives before evaluating them. Pick one idea that changes a meaningful assumption, then make the smallest sketch, prototype, or experiment that could test it. State what you expect to learn, what result would count against it, and the time or resource limit. Preserve the observation from a failed test even if you discard the solution.

**Keep in mind.** Borrowing from another field is a prompt, not evidence that its solution will transfer. Explain what is similar about the mechanism and what differs about the constraints.

### Questions

- What rule or assumption would you break just to see what new ideas emerge?

- Which failed attempt contains a useful observation we have not yet used?

- What could we combine from two unrelated approaches to create another option?

- How would someone encountering this problem for the first time describe it?

- If time and resources were temporarily unconstrained, what would we try? Which part survives when the real constraints return?

- Which part of an apparently impossible idea can we test cheaply?

> Source: Adapted from the Notion Creativity section.

## Listening and Dialogue

**Use when.** When two people use the same words but reach different conclusions, when a report becomes guarded, or before giving advice on a situation you do not yet understand.

**After the answer.** Check your summary with the speaker before responding. If the disagreement is factual, identify the observation to verify. If it concerns goals or constraints, name the difference explicitly. Ask what help is wanted and agree the next step; if no action is wanted yet, respect that and make no promise on their behalf.

**Keep in mind.** Replace an accusatory "why did you" with an invitation to explain what led to the action. Keep personal questions optional and work-relevant. Understanding a perspective does not require agreeing with it; use the Conflict bank if interests remain incompatible.

### Questions

- Here is what I think happened and why. What information am I missing?

- Can you walk me from the observation to the conclusion you reached?

- Let me restate what I heard. What have I misunderstood or left out?

- What would help you feel understood in this conversation?

- Before I suggest a solution, is there another part of this that you want me to understand?

> Source: Adapted from the Notion Connecting with Others section, Questioning via Dialogue (Exhibit 9.2) in its Leading with Questions screenshots, and Berger listening prompts. The dialogue exhibit itself credits Bianco-Mathis, Nabors and Roman.

## Self-Assessment

**Use when.** In a weekly reflection, after a difficult interaction, or before making a judgment about someone else. Choose one question connected to an actual event.

**After the answer.** Write the event and the behavior you want to change. Ask for another perspective where appropriate, acknowledge your part specifically, and choose one observable change. Revisit the same situation or behavior at a stated date. If you caused harm, reflection should lead to repair with the person affected, not only a private resolution.

**Keep in mind.** Use a successful event too: identify the conditions worth repeating. Your intention to be fair or caring is not evidence of its effect; look for how decisions and behavior landed with the people affected.

### Questions

- What did you think this job was going to be, and how does it differ from what it actually is?

- In what situations do you still default to individual contributor mode? What's the cost of that?

- Who do you talk to honestly about how the job is going? If nobody, why not?

- What's the failure mode you're most worried about in yourself right now?

- What feedback have you received that you initially dismissed and later realized was accurate?

- What are you most defensive about? What does that defensiveness protect?

- What's the thing you're most likely to be wrong about in your current role?

- Who challenges your thinking in ways that have actually changed your mind?

- Is the pace at which you're currently operating sustainable for another year?

- What would you stop doing if you had to free up 20% of your time?

- Do people feel safe with me? What have they told me, and what have I observed when they disagree or report a mistake?

- Do I allow my team to make mistakes? How do they react when they do?

- Am I motivated by the work itself, or primarily by how it looks?

<!-- pagebreak -->

### Self-Assessment (continued)

- What's something I believed about leadership two years ago that I now think was wrong?

- How can I improve? What are my shortcomings? How can I help one of my reports today?

- Am I spending more time with my struggling performers than my strongest ones? What is the cost of that imbalance?

- When did I last make a mistake that affected my team? Did I go back and acknowledge it specifically - not vaguely - and reconnect?

- Would my reports gladly work for me again? What evidence supports my answer, and how could I invite an honest view without putting them on the spot?

- Why might my current approach not be working - even if it has worked before?

- How many of my team's successes have I celebrated recently, and was the recognition specific enough to be meaningful?

- Where did I apply different standards to similar situations, and can I justify the difference?

- What support should I ask my team for instead of pretending I already know?

- Which recent action showed someone that I care about their work and circumstances?

- Where did I leave enough room for someone else to decide, and where did I take that room back?

- Before I criticize, what useful change am I seeking, and what part of this problem is mine to own?

- Which recent success could we learn from, and what conditions made it possible?

> Source: Existing handbook bank plus adapted prompts from Ratipcan Uysal's Notion section marked 'my additions', its appreciative-inquiry passage, and the Berger pre-criticism image.

## Conflict

**Use when.** When there is a concrete disagreement about goals, resources, expectations, or behavior. Establish what you are authorized to resolve and whether separate conversations are needed first.

**After the answer.** Summarize each side in terms they recognize. Separate shared interests from incompatible requirements. Agree objective criteria for options, then write the decision, the owner of each action, and when the parties will check whether the agreement is working. If agreement is impossible, name the unresolved trade-off and the decision-maker.

**Keep in mind.** Do not force a mutual-compromise exercise for a report that needs a formal workplace process. Listening is useful, but it does not replace accountability or an appropriate escalation. See Chapter 16.

### Questions

- How can we separate the people from the problem - diagnose the cause of the conflict without making it personal?

- What goals are actually in conflict here?

- What does each side want - and what do they need underneath that want?

- Do all sides have a clear understanding of the issues as the other side sees them?

- How can I encourage each side to view the conflict from the other side's perspective and to practice active listening?

- What are the common areas of interest between the two sides?

- What are the underlying issues - not just each side's stated position?

- How can each side get what it needs without requiring the other to lose?

- What are the issues that are genuinely incompatible between the two sides?

- What are the most important goals of each side - the ones they will not compromise on?

- How can we generate options that could solve the problem - options neither side has considered yet?

- What objective criteria will we use as a basis for our decisions, so the outcome isn't determined by who has more power?

- How can we disagree in an agreeable fashion - maintain the relationship while resolving the substance?

> Source: Existing handbook bank, also present in the Notion Leading with Questions conflict image, attributed there to William Ury.

## The Manager Event Loop

**Use when.** During daily planning, a weekly review, or a monthly check. Pick the relevant cadence and domain.

**After the answer.** Put a needed action in the team's existing calendar or task system, with an owner and date. Check earlier promises before adding new ones. A recurring obstacle may require a process or responsibility change.

**Keep in mind.** Skip irrelevant items. Adapt the software-production check to the work your team actually does; do not create a second reporting system.

### People

- Daily: Do I need to check in on someone? Any recruiting decisions needed quickly?

- Weekly: Did I promise someone a follow-up last week? Do I have a 1-on-1 scheduled with everyone?

- Monthly: Is everyone aware of the expectations for next month? How is team morale on average?

### Project

- Daily: Are all projects/epics on track? Are any tickets blocked because of me?

- Weekly: Do I need to update anyone on project status? Do new features need a production check?

- Monthly: Any projects on the back burner that should be picked up? Do all projects still make sense, or is sunk cost keeping something alive?

### Process

- Daily: Anything that needs to be discussed at stand-up? Have I written my goals for tomorrow?

- Weekly: Are the tickets groomed for next week? Any action items from the retro assigned to me?

- Monthly: Is the process too bloated - can we automate something? What could we do differently? What's working well that we should do more of? Do we need to stop and correct course?

### Me

- Daily: Am I prepared for my meetings - can any be cancelled? How am I feeling on a scale of 1-10? Am I delegating enough?

- Weekly: How was my progress this week? Did I carve out time to learn something new? Can I actually disconnect from work at the end of the week?

- Monthly: Am I living up to my expectations? How can I create more flow at work? Am I available enough for my team?

> Source: Existing handbook cadence, completed from the Manager - Event Loop image in Notion. The image does not identify an author.

## Measuring Yourself as a Manager

**Use when.** At an agreed feedback interval and after people have had time to experience a management change. Explain the purpose, who can see responses, and how results will be used before collecting them.

**After the answer.** Look for themes and concrete examples. Discuss the themes without trying to identify anonymous respondents. Choose one behavior to improve, tell the team what you will do and by when, and return with what changed. If the feedback is unclear, offer a safe route for clarification rather than pressuring individuals to justify a score.

**Keep in mind.** The statements below are the handbook's existing adaptation of the Google survey reproduced in Notion. They are not a newly validated scale. Do not infer precision from a small group, promise anonymity you cannot provide, or turn a single score into a verdict on a person.

### Questions

- My manager gives me actionable feedback that helps me improve my performance.

- My manager does not micromanage (get involved in details that should be handled at other levels).

- My manager shows consideration for me as a person.

- The actions of my manager show that he/she values the perspective I bring to the team, even if it is different from his/her own.

- My manager keeps the team focused on our priority results and deliverables.

- My manager regularly shares relevant information from his/her manager and senior leaders.

- My manager has had a meaningful discussion with me about career development in the past six months.

- My manager communicates clear goals for our team.

- My manager has the technical expertise required to effectively manage me.

- I would recommend my manager to other team members.

- I am satisfied with my manager's overall performance as a manager.

- Open: What would you recommend your manager keep doing?

- Open: What would you have your manager change?

> Source: Existing handbook instrument based on the Google Project Oxygen survey as reproduced in Notion; the use and follow-through guidance is editorial.
